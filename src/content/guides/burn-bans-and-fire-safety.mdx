Bastrop County has a long memory of wildfire. That is why the burn ban sits in the header of this site, next to the weather. The header is a reading of a county page. It is not the order. This guide explains how to read the order, and which statewide rules still apply when the county has not banned burning.

## How a burn ban works

The county posts burn-ban status on its outdoor-burning page. When this guide was updated, on November 22, 2026, that page said there is no burn ban in effect. The same page keeps PDFs of earlier orders. The dates on those files include September 28, September 14, and August 24, 2026, and March 23, March 9, and February 13, 2026, plus orders from September and March 2025. The page also lists a January 16, 2026, declaration of disaster for the threat of wildfires. Those files are history. They show how often the status moves. They are not today’s permission.

Bastropia copies the county status onto the [burn-ban page](/burn-ban). If the county page and this site disagree, believe the county page. You can also ask for an email or a text when the desk’s reading changes, from the [alerts page](/alerts).

The Texas A&M Forest Service publishes a statewide burn-ban map. The county links it from the outdoor-burning page. A statewide map is a second look, not a substitute for the county order. The county page also links a wildfire-risk tool and the county’s community wildfire protection plan. Use those links on the county page if you want the risk for a specific address. This guide does not restate a risk score, because that number changes with the weather.

## The outdoor-burning rule

A day with no burn ban is not a day when every fire is legal.

The county’s outdoor-burning page describes the TCEQ rule this way: it first prohibits outdoor burning anywhere in Texas, and then allows exceptions for specific situations. The county points readers to TCEQ’s publication RG-049, “Outdoor Burning in Texas,” for the exceptions and the conditions. TCEQ’s summary of that publication says the agency generally does not issue burn permits, and that a person who needs to burn outside the listed exceptions can ask TCEQ for a special authorization. The person who starts a fire remains liable for the damage, even when the burn meets the rule.

The general requirements, in the rule text the state plan carries as 30 TAC 111.219, are the practical part. They apply when a section of the rule says they apply. In plain language, from that text:

Burning has to be outside the city limits unless that city has an ordinance that allows it, consistent with the Texas Clean Air Act. Smoke cannot be allowed to create a hazard for a public road, a landing strip, or navigable water, or to harm an off-site structure that contains what the rule calls sensitive receptors, such as a home. If smoke blows onto a road, the person who started the fire has to post flaggers. The fire has to be downwind of, or at least 300 feet from, a neighboring structure with sensitive receptors, unless the neighbor who lives or works there has given written approval.

Do not start earlier than one hour after sunrise. Finish the same day, no later than one hour before sunset. A responsible person has to be there while the fire is still advancing. Do not start if the predicted surface wind is under 6 miles per hour or over 23 miles per hour. Do not burn during a persistent low-level temperature inversion.

Some materials are banned outright in that section: electrical insulation, treated lumber, plastics, non-wood construction and demolition materials, heavy oils, asphaltic materials, potentially explosive materials, chemical wastes, and items containing natural or synthetic rubber. Household trash is not a brush pile. Tires, treated wood, and shingles are in that prohibited list.

Prescribed or controlled burning for forest management has an extra step. The rule says to notify the Texas A&M Forest Service beforehand.

Which everyday burns qualify as an exception, such as on-site brush from land clearing, is spelled out in RG-049. This guide does not paraphrase every exception. If your pile does not clearly match one, do not light it. Ask TCEQ or the county Office of Emergency Management before you treat a neighbor’s habit as the rule.

## Inside a city, a park, or a fire district

City limits change the rule. Section 111.219 says burning must be outside the corporate limits unless the city has adopted an ordinance that permits burning. Bastrop, Elgin, and Smithville are incorporated. A county page that says “no burn ban” does not, by itself, authorize a fire inside those cities. Read the city ordinance or call city hall before you burn inside the city limits. Paige is unincorporated, so it does not have a city burn ordinance of its own. The county order and the TCEQ rule are what apply there. The [Paige guide](/guides/paige) has the rest of what it means to live outside a city.

State parks add their own campfire rules. The Bastrop State Park map says that when a burn ban is not in effect, campfires are allowed only in the fire rings the park provides, and that gathering firewood is prohibited. Ground fires are not allowed. A campfire ring is not permission to burn a debris pile.

Bastrop County ESD No. 2 publishes its own outdoor-burning notes. If you are in that district, read that page as well as the county order. The district’s number for outdoor-burning questions is on the ESD page and on this site’s [burn-ban page](/burn-ban).

## Who to call

If a fire is out of the ring or moving into grass, call 911. Do not stop to check a website.

For the status of an order, use the county outdoor-burning page. The Office of Emergency Management is at 1501 Business Park Drive, Bastrop, TX 78602, phone (512) 581-4022. Its mailing address is 804 Pecan Street. The OEM page also tells residents to register with Warn Central Texas so the county can reach a phone, email, or text during a disaster, and to turn on smartphone emergency alerts.

The same OEM page links preparedness notes, including wildfire weather and defensible space. Those are short public articles, not a contractor’s estimate. Start there if you want the county’s own checklist for the house.

The [government guide](/guides/bastrop-county-government) covers Commissioners Court, which is the body that can adopt a burn-ban order. The [things-to-do guide](/guides/things-to-do-in-bastrop-county) covers the parks, where the campfire rule and the county order meet. Neither one replaces the county page on the day you burn.
